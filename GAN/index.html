1. Goal of Generative Modeling

Suppose we are given a dataset \[ \mathcal{D} = \{x_1,x_2,\ldots,x_n\}, \] where the data points are sampled from an unknown data distribution \(p_X\).

Goal:

During inference, we would like to generate new samples that are indistinguishable from samples drawn from the true data distribution.

\[ x \sim p_X \]

However, during inference we do not have direct access to the true distribution \(p_X\). Therefore, we need to learn a model distribution \(p_\theta\) that is as similar as possible to \(p_X\).

\[ p_\theta \approx p_X \]

Once we have learned such a distribution, we should be able to generate samples from \(p_\theta\) that resemble samples from the original data distribution.

2. The GAN Approach

A natural approach would be to explicitly model the probability distribution \(p_X\). However, as we saw with other generative models, explicitly modeling a complicated high-dimensional data distribution can be difficult.

The key idea behind GANs:

Instead of explicitly modeling the probability density \(p_X\), a GAN learns a generator that can directly produce samples that resemble samples from the true data distribution.

We start with a simple, known random distribution and transform samples from this distribution using a neural network called the generator.

\[ z \sim \mathcal{N}(0,I) \]

The generator \(g_\theta\) maps the latent noise \(z\) to a generated sample:

\[ \hat{x} = g_\theta(z). \]

Therefore, instead of directly sampling from the unknown distribution \(p_X\), we sample a simple latent variable \(z\) and pass it through the generator.

\(z\)
Random noise
→
\(g_\theta(z)\)
Generator
→
\(\hat{x}\)
Generated sample

If the generator learns the true data distribution sufficiently well, then the generated samples should become indistinguishable from real samples.

\[ p_\theta = p_X \]

3. Introducing the Discriminator

Simply generating samples is not enough. We also need a way to determine whether the generated samples are realistic.

To do this, GANs introduce another neural network called the discriminator.

What does the discriminator do?

The discriminator receives a sample and predicts whether it is real or fake.

\[ f_\phi(x) \in [0,1] \]

We interpret \(f_\phi(x)\) as the probability that the input \(x\) is a real sample.

Real sample

For a real training example \(x\sim p_X\), the desired label is \[ y=1. \]

Generated sample

For a generated example \[ \hat{x}=g_\theta(z), \] the desired label is \[ y=0. \]

Thus, the discriminator is trained as a binary classifier whose job is to distinguish real samples from generated samples.

4. Training the Discriminator

Since the discriminator performs binary classification, we use the binary cross-entropy loss.

\[ \mathcal{L}_D(\phi) = - \sum_i \log \left[ f_\phi(x_i) \right] - \sum_j \log \left[ 1-f_\phi(\hat{x}_j) \right]. \]

Here, the real examples \(x_i\) have target label \(1\), while the generated examples \(\hat{x}_j\) have target label \(0\).

\[ \phi^* = \arg\min_\phi \mathcal{L}_D(\phi). \]

In other words, the discriminator tries to assign a high probability to real samples and a low probability to generated samples.

Discriminator's objective:

Correctly classify real samples as real and generated samples as fake.

5. Training the Generator

Now consider the generator. The generator produces \[ \hat{x}_j=g_\theta(z_j). \]

The generator has a different objective: it wants the discriminator to classify its generated samples as real.

Generator's objective:

Produce samples that fool the discriminator into believing that they are real.

Therefore, while training the generator, we want to maximize the discriminator's output on generated samples.

\[ \theta^* = \arg\max_\theta \sum_j \log f_\phi \left[ g_\theta(z_j) \right]. \]

This leads naturally to the adversarial interaction between the generator and discriminator.

6. The GAN Minimax Game

The discriminator tries to correctly distinguish real and generated samples, while the generator tries to fool the discriminator.

These two objectives oppose each other, resulting in a minimax game.

\[ \boxed{ \min_\theta \max_\phi \; V(\theta,\phi) } \]

The original GAN objective can be written as

\[ \boxed{ \min_G\max_D V(D,G) = \mathbb{E}_{x\sim p_X} [\log D(x)] + \mathbb{E}_{z\sim p(z)} [ \log(1-D(G(z))) ] } \]
Think of GAN training as a game:
  • The discriminator tries to distinguish real from fake samples.
  • The generator tries to generate samples that the discriminator cannot distinguish from real data.

7. GAN Training

GAN training alternates between updating the discriminator and updating the generator.

Sample \(z\)
\(z\sim\mathcal{N}(0,I)\)
→
Generate \(\hat{x}\)
\(\hat{x}=G_\theta(z)\)
→
Discriminate
Real / Fake

At each training step:

  1. Draw a batch of latent variables \(z\sim\mathcal{N}(0,I)\).
  2. Pass the latent variables through the generator to obtain \[ \hat{x}=G_\theta(z). \]
  3. Choose a batch of real training examples \(x\).
  4. Pass both real and generated examples through the discriminator.
  5. Calculate the discriminator loss and backpropagate.
  6. Update the generator according to its adversarial objective.

11. Analysis of the GAN Loss Function

To analyze the GAN objective more carefully, suppose we divide the loss according to the number of real and generated samples.

In expectation form, the discriminator loss can be written as

\[ \mathcal{L}(\phi) = - \mathbb{E}_{\hat{x}\sim p_\theta} \left[ \log \left( 1-D_\phi(\hat{x}) \right) \right] - \mathbb{E}_{x\sim p_X} \left[ \log D_\phi(x) \right]. \]

Equivalently, using the corresponding probability densities:

\[ \mathcal{L}(\phi) = - \int p_\theta(\hat{x}) \log \left[ 1-D_\phi(\hat{x}) \right] d\hat{x} - \int p_X(x) \log D_\phi(x) dx. \]

We can now ask an important question:

What is the optimal discriminator?

For a fixed generator, what value of the discriminator output \(D(x)\) minimizes the discriminator loss?

12. Finding the Optimal Discriminator

Let \[ p_X(x)=A, \qquad p_\theta(x)=B, \] and denote the discriminator output at \(x\) by \[ D(x)=D. \]

The contribution to the discriminator objective can then be written as

\[ -B\log(1-D)-A\log D. \]

To find the optimal discriminator, differentiate with respect to \(D\) and set the derivative to zero:

\[ \frac{\partial}{\partial D} \left[ -B\log(1-D)-A\log D \right] =0. \]
\[ \frac{B}{1-D} - \frac{A}{D} =0. \]

Rearranging:

\[ BD=A(1-D) \] \[ (A+B)D=A. \]
\[ \boxed{ D^*(x) = \frac{p_X(x)} {p_X(x)+p_\theta(x)} } \]
Key result:

For a fixed generator, the optimal discriminator compares the density of real data with the density of generated data.

13. What Happens at the Optimal Discriminator?

Substituting the optimal discriminator \[ D^*(x) = \frac{p_X(x)} {p_X(x)+p_\theta(x)} \] back into the GAN objective gives

\[ \begin{aligned} V(D^*,G) &= \int p_X(x) \log \left[ \frac{p_X(x)} {p_X(x)+p_\theta(x)} \right] dx \\[6pt] &\quad+ \int p_\theta(x) \log \left[ \frac{p_\theta(x)} {p_X(x)+p_\theta(x)} \right] dx. \end{aligned} \]
