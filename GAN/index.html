Generative Adversarial Networks

Understanding generative modeling through the adversarial learning framework.

1. Goal of Generative Modeling

Suppose we are given a dataset

\[ \mathcal{D}=\left\{x^{(i)}\right\}_{i=1}^{N}, \qquad x^{(i)}\sim_{\mathrm{iid}}p_X(x). \]

where \(p_X(x)\) denotes the unknown data distribution.

Goal:

During inference, we would like to generate new samples that are indistinguishable from samples drawn from the true data distribution, \(x\sim p_X\).

However, during inference we do not have direct access to the true distribution \(p_X\). Therefore, we need to learn a model distribution \(p_\theta\) that approximates \(p_X\) as closely as possible, \(p_\theta\approx p_X\).

Once we have learned such a model, we should be able to generate samples from \(p_\theta\) that resemble samples from the original data distribution.

2. The GAN Approach

A natural approach would be to explicitly model the probability distribution \(p_X\). However, as we have seen with other generative models, explicitly modeling a complicated, high-dimensional data distribution can be difficult.

The key idea behind GANs:

Instead of explicitly modeling a tractable probability density, a GAN learns a generator that directly produces samples resembling those from the true data distribution.

We start with a simple, known random distribution and transform samples from this distribution using a neural network called the generator.

\[z\sim\mathcal{N}(0,I).\]

The generator \(G_\theta\) maps the latent noise \(z\) to a generated sample:

\[ \tilde{x}=G_\theta(z). \]

Therefore, instead of directly sampling from the unknown distribution \(p_X\), we sample a simple latent variable \(z\) and pass it through the generator.

\(z\)
Random noise
→
\(G_\theta(z)\)
Generator
→
\(\tilde{x}\)
Generated sample

If the generator learns the true data distribution sufficiently well, its generated samples should become indistinguishable from real samples. In the ideal case, the generator-induced distribution \(p_\theta\) matches the data distribution, \(p_\theta=p_X\).

3. Introducing the Discriminator

Simply generating samples is not enough. We also need a way to determine whether the generated samples are realistic.

To do this, GANs introduce another neural network called the discriminator.

What does the discriminator do?

The discriminator receives a sample and predicts whether it is real or generated.

\[D_\phi(x)\in[0,1].\]

We interpret \(D_\phi(x)\) as the discriminator's estimated probability that the input \(x\) is a real sample.

Real sample

For a real training example \(x\sim p_X\), the desired label is

\[y=1.\]

Generated sample

For a generated example \(\tilde{x}=G_\theta(z)\), the desired label is

\[y=0.\]

Thus, the discriminator is trained as a binary classifier whose task is to distinguish real samples from generated samples.

4. Training the Discriminator

Since the discriminator performs binary classification, we use the binary cross-entropy loss.

Suppose we have real samples \(x_i\sim p_X\) and generated samples \(\tilde{x}_j=G_\theta(z_j)\). The discriminator loss is

\[ \mathcal{L}_D(\phi) = -\sum_i\log D_\phi(x_i) -\sum_j\log\left(1-D_\phi(\tilde{x}_j)\right). \]

Here, the real examples \(x_i\) have target label \(1\), while the generated examples \(\tilde{x}_j\) have target label \(0\).

\[ \phi^*=\arg\min_\phi\mathcal{L}_D(\phi). \]

In other words, the discriminator tries to assign a high probability to real samples and a low probability to generated samples.

Discriminator's objective:

Correctly classify real samples as real and generated samples as generated.

5. Training the Generator

Now consider the generator. It produces

\[ \tilde{x}_j=G_\theta(z_j). \]

The generator has a different objective: it wants the discriminator to classify its generated samples as real.

Generator's objective:

Produce samples that fool the discriminator into believing that they are real.

Therefore, while training the generator, we want to maximize the discriminator's output on generated samples.

\[ \theta^* = \arg\max_\theta \sum_j \log D_\phi\!\left(G_\theta(z_j)\right). \]

This creates the adversarial interaction between the generator and discriminator: the generator improves by learning to fool the discriminator, while the discriminator improves by learning to detect generated samples.

6. The GAN Minimax Game

The discriminator tries to correctly distinguish real and generated samples, while the generator tries to fool the discriminator.

These two objectives oppose each other, resulting in a minimax game.

\[ \boxed{\min_\theta\max_\phi V(\theta,\phi)} \]

The original GAN objective can be written as

\[ \boxed{ \begin{aligned} \min_\theta\max_\phi V(\theta,\phi) &= \mathbb{E}_{x\sim p_X} \left[\log D_\phi(x)\right]\\ &\quad+ \mathbb{E}_{z\sim p(z)} \left[ \log\left(1-D_\phi(G_\theta(z))\right) \right]. \end{aligned}} \]
Think of GAN training as a game:
  • The discriminator tries to distinguish real samples from generated samples.
  • The generator tries to generate samples that the discriminator cannot distinguish from real data.

7. GAN Training

GAN training alternates between updating the discriminator and updating the generator.

Sample \(z\)
\(z\sim\mathcal{N}(0,I)\)
→
Generate \(\tilde{x}\)
\(\tilde{x}=G_\theta(z)\)
→
Discriminate
Real / Generated

At each training step:

  1. Draw a batch of latent variables \(z\sim\mathcal{N}(0,I)\).
  2. Pass the latent variables through the generator to obtain \(\tilde{x}=G_\theta(z)\).
  3. Choose a batch of real training examples \(x\).
  4. Pass both real and generated examples through the discriminator.
  5. Calculate the discriminator loss and backpropagate through the discriminator.
  6. Update the generator according to its adversarial objective.
Key idea:

The discriminator and generator are trained against each other. The discriminator learns to improve its ability to distinguish real from generated samples, while the generator learns to produce increasingly realistic samples.

11. Analysis of the GAN Loss Function

To analyze the GAN objective more carefully, consider the discriminator loss in expectation form.

\[ \mathcal{L}_D(\phi) = -\mathbb{E}_{\tilde{x}\sim p_\theta} \left[ \log\left(1-D_\phi(\tilde{x})\right) \right] -\mathbb{E}_{x\sim p_X} \left[ \log D_\phi(x) \right]. \]

Equivalently, using the corresponding probability densities, the loss can be written as

\[ \mathcal{L}_D(\phi) = -\int p_\theta(x)\log\left[1-D_\phi(x)\right]\,dx -\int p_X(x)\log D_\phi(x)\,dx. \]

We can now ask an important question:

What is the optimal discriminator?

For a fixed generator, what value of the discriminator output \(D_\phi(x)\) minimizes the discriminator loss?

12. Finding the Optimal Discriminator

Let

\[ p_X(x)=A, \qquad p_\theta(x)=B, \qquad D_\phi(x)=D. \]

At a fixed \(x\), the contribution to the discriminator loss can then be written as

\[ -B\log(1-D)-A\log D. \]

To find the optimal discriminator, differentiate with respect to \(D\) and set the derivative to zero:

\[ \frac{\partial}{\partial D} \left[ -B\log(1-D)-A\log D \right] =0. \]
\[ \frac{B}{1-D}-\frac{A}{D}=0. \]

Rearranging gives

\[ BD=A(1-D), \] \[ (A+B)D=A. \]
Optimal discriminator
\[ \boxed{ D^*(x)= \frac{p_X(x)} {p_X(x)+p_\theta(x)} } \]
Key result:

For a fixed generator, the optimal discriminator compares the density of real data with the density of generated data.

13. What Happens at the Optimal Discriminator?

Substituting the optimal discriminator

\[ D^*(x)= \frac{p_X(x)} {p_X(x)+p_\theta(x)} \]

back into the GAN objective gives

\[ \begin{aligned} V(D^*,G_\theta) &= \int p_X(x) \log \left[ \frac{p_X(x)} {p_X(x)+p_\theta(x)} \right]dx\\[6pt] &\quad+ \int p_\theta(x) \log \left[ \frac{p_\theta(x)} {p_X(x)+p_\theta(x)} \right]dx. \end{aligned} \]
