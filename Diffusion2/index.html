Diffusion Models II: Noise, Scores, Guidance & DDIM

The Big Picture

In Part 1 we derived the DDPM: a fixed noising process, a learned denoising process, and an ELBO that reduces to a regression \(\|\hat{x}_\theta(x_t) - x_0\|_2^2\) onto the clean data point. This lesson builds on that result in four directions:

Alternate interpretations

The same network can predict the added noise \(\epsilon_t\) or the score \(\nabla_{x_t}\log p(x_t)\). All three targets are equivalent.

Guided diffusion

Sample from \(p(x_0\mid y)\) by steering the score with a classifier, or without one (classifier-free guidance).

Latent diffusion

Run the diffusion in the compact latent space of a pre-trained encoder–decoder instead of in pixel space.

DDIM

A non-Markovian family with the same training as DDPM but a deterministic, invertible sampler.

1. DDPM as Regression over the Added Noise

Recall the forward process of a DDPM, which lets us jump from \(x_0\) to any \(x_t\) directly:

\[ x_t = \sqrt{\bar\alpha_t}\,x_0 + \sqrt{1-\bar\alpha_t}\,\epsilon_t, \qquad \epsilon_t \sim \mathcal{N}(0, I). \]

Rearranging the terms,

Clean data in terms of the noise
\[ \boxed{ x_0 = \frac{x_t - \sqrt{1-\bar\alpha_t}\,\epsilon_t}{\sqrt{\bar\alpha_t}} } \tag{1} \]

Here \(\epsilon_t\) is the noise that was added to \(x_0\) to convert it into \(x_t\).

The consistency (denoising matching) term in the ELBO is \(\|\mu_\theta - \mu_q\|_2^2\), where

  • \(\mu_q\): the mean of the known posterior \(q(x_{t-1}\mid x_t, x_0)\),
  • \(\mu_\theta\): the mean of the learned reverse step \(p_\theta(x_{t-1}\mid x_t)\).

1.1 Writing \(\mu_q\) in terms of \(x_t\) and \(\epsilon_t\)

From Part 1,

\[ \mu_q(x_t, x_0) = \frac{\sqrt{\alpha_t}\,(1-\bar\alpha_{t-1})\,x_t + \sqrt{\bar\alpha_{t-1}}\,(1-\alpha_t)\,x_0} {1-\bar\alpha_t}. \]

Replacing \(x_0\) using Eq. (1):

\[ \mu_q(x_t, x_0) = \frac{\sqrt{\alpha_t}\,(1-\bar\alpha_{t-1})\,x_t + \sqrt{\bar\alpha_{t-1}}\,(1-\alpha_t) \overbrace{\left(\dfrac{x_t - \sqrt{1-\bar\alpha_t}\,\epsilon_t}{\sqrt{\bar\alpha_t}}\right)}^{x_0}} {1-\bar\alpha_t}. \]

Using \(\sqrt{\bar\alpha_{t-1}}/\sqrt{\bar\alpha_t} = 1/\sqrt{\alpha_t}\), the coefficient of \(x_t\) becomes \(\frac{\alpha_t(1-\bar\alpha_{t-1}) + 1-\alpha_t}{\sqrt{\alpha_t}\,(1-\bar\alpha_t)} = \frac{1}{\sqrt{\alpha_t}}\), and so

The posterior mean in terms of the noise
\[ \boxed{ \mu_q(x_t, \epsilon_t) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{\alpha_t}}\,x_t - \frac{1-\alpha_t}{\sqrt{1-\bar\alpha_t}\,\sqrt{\alpha_t}}\,\epsilon_t } \]

1.2 Parameterizing \(\mu_\theta\) with a noise predictor

As in Part 1, the network already sees \(x_t\), so we give \(\mu_\theta\) the same form and only replace the unknown, which is now the noise:

\[ \mu_\theta(x_t) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{\alpha_t}}\,x_t - \frac{1-\alpha_t}{\sqrt{1-\bar\alpha_t}\,\sqrt{\alpha_t}}\,\hat\epsilon_\theta(x_t), \]

where \(\hat\epsilon_\theta(x_t)\) is the output of a neural network. The \(x_t\) terms cancel in the consistency term:

\[ \begin{aligned} \text{Consistency term} &= \frac{1}{2\sigma_q^2}\,\|\mu_q - \mu_\theta\|_2^2 \\[6pt] &= \frac{1}{2\sigma_q^2}\, \frac{(1-\alpha_t)^2}{(1-\bar\alpha_t)\,\alpha_t} \left[\,\|\epsilon_t - \hat\epsilon_\theta(x_t)\|_2^2\,\right] \\[6pt] &\propto \|\epsilon_t - \hat\epsilon_\theta(x_t)\|_2^2. \end{aligned} \]
Regression over the added noise. Instead of predicting the clean image, the network predicts the noise that was mixed into it. This is the parameterization used in the original DDPM paper and in most diffusion models in practice.
\(x_t,\ t\) noisy input + time
→
U-Net \(\theta\) decrease dim → increase dim
→
\(\hat\epsilon_\theta(x_t)\) regressor on the added noise

Trained with \(\|\epsilon_t - \hat\epsilon_\theta(x_t)\|_2^2\).

2. DDPM as a Score Predictor

2.1 Tweedie's formula

Suppose a random variable \(z\) is Gaussian with mean \(\mu_z\) and covariance \(\Sigma_z\):

\[ z \sim \mathcal{N}(z;\ \mu_z,\ \Sigma_z) = p(z). \]

Tweedie's formula says that, having observed \(z\), the best estimate of its mean is

Tweedie's formula
\[ \boxed{ \mathbb{E}\left[\mu_z \mid z\right] = z + \Sigma_z\,\nabla_z \log p(z) } \]

where \(\nabla_z \log p(z)\) is defined as the score function: the direction in which the log-density increases fastest.

2.2 Applying Tweedie's formula to the forward process

Recall that in a DDPM,

\[ q(x_t\mid x_0) = \mathcal{N}\!\left(x_t;\ \underbrace{\sqrt{\bar\alpha_t}\,x_0}_{\text{mean}},\ (1-\bar\alpha_t)\,I\right). \]

Applying Tweedie's formula with \(z = x_t\) and \(\Sigma_z = (1-\bar\alpha_t)\,I\):

\[ \mathbb{E}\left[\mu \mid x_t\right] = x_t + (1-\bar\alpha_t)\,\nabla_{x_t}\log p(x_t). \]

The best estimate for \(\mathbb{E}[\mu\mid x_t]\) is the true mean, \(\mathbb{E}[\mu\mid x_t] = \sqrt{\bar\alpha_t}\,x_0\). Therefore

\[ \sqrt{\bar\alpha_t}\,x_0 = x_t + (1-\bar\alpha_t)\,\nabla_{x_t}\log p(x_t). \]

Rearranging the terms,

Clean data in terms of the score
\[ \boxed{ x_0 = \frac{x_t + (1-\bar\alpha_t)\,\nabla_{x_t}\log p(x_t)}{\sqrt{\bar\alpha_t}} } \tag{2} \]

2.3 The consistency term in terms of the score

Substituting Eq. (2) for \(x_0\) in \(\mu_q\):

\[ \mu_q(x_t, x_0) = \frac{\sqrt{\alpha_t}\,(1-\bar\alpha_{t-1})\,x_t + \sqrt{\bar\alpha_{t-1}}\,(1-\alpha_t) \overbrace{\left(\dfrac{x_t + (1-\bar\alpha_t)\,\nabla_{x_t}\log p(x_t)}{\sqrt{\bar\alpha_t}}\right)}^{x_0}} {1-\bar\alpha_t}. \]
The posterior mean in terms of the score
\[ \boxed{ \mu_q = \frac{1}{\sqrt{\alpha_t}}\,x_t + \frac{1-\alpha_t}{\sqrt{\alpha_t}}\,\nabla_{x_t}\log p(x_t) } \]

We parameterize the model mean the same way, with a network \(s_\theta(x_t)\) that outputs the predicted score:

\[ \mu_\theta = \frac{1}{\sqrt{\alpha_t}}\,x_t + \frac{1-\alpha_t}{\sqrt{\alpha_t}}\,s_\theta(x_t). \]

Then

\[ \text{Consistency term} \;\propto\; \|\mu_q - \mu_\theta\|_2^2 \;\propto\; \left\|\nabla_{x_t}\log p(x_t) - s_\theta(x_t)\right\|_2^2. \]
Regression over the score function. Training a DDPM can equally be seen as teaching a network to predict the score of the noisy data distribution at every noise level.
\(x_t,\ t\) noisy input + time
→
U-Net \(\theta\) decrease dim → increase dim
→
\(s_\theta(x_t)\) regressor on the score function

Trained with \(\|\nabla_{x_t}\log p(x_t) - s_\theta(x_t)\|_2^2\).

3. Computing the True Score: Score = Scaled Noise

We now have two expressions for \(x_0\):

\[ x_0 = \frac{x_t + (1-\bar\alpha_t)\,\nabla_{x_t}\log p(x_t)}{\sqrt{\bar\alpha_t}} \quad \text{(from Tweedie, Eq. 2)}, \] \[ x_0 = \frac{x_t - \sqrt{1-\bar\alpha_t}\,\epsilon_t}{\sqrt{\bar\alpha_t}} \quad \text{(by definition, Eq. 1)}, \qquad \epsilon_t \sim \mathcal{N}(0, I). \]

Equating the two and rearranging, \((1-\bar\alpha_t)\,\nabla_{x_t}\log p(x_t) = -\sqrt{1-\bar\alpha_t}\,\epsilon_t\), so

True score = negatively scaled noise
\[ \boxed{ \nabla_{x_t}\log p(x_t) = -\frac{1}{\sqrt{1-\bar\alpha_t}}\,\epsilon_t } \]
Why this makes sense: the noise \(\epsilon_t\) points away from the clean data. The score points towards regions of higher probability, i.e. back towards the data. So the score is the noise with its sign flipped and rescaled.

Therefore, a DDPM trained to regress over the added noise is implicitly predicting the score function: \(s_\theta(x_t) = -\hat\epsilon_\theta(x_t)/\sqrt{1-\bar\alpha_t}\).

3.1 Three equivalent views

The network predicts Regression target Conversion
Clean data (Part 1) \(\|\hat{x}_\theta(x_t) - x_0\|_2^2\) \(x_0 = \frac{x_t - \sqrt{1-\bar\alpha_t}\,\epsilon_t}{\sqrt{\bar\alpha_t}}\)
Added noise \(\|\epsilon_t - \hat\epsilon_\theta(x_t)\|_2^2\) \(\epsilon_t = \frac{x_t - \sqrt{\bar\alpha_t}\,x_0}{\sqrt{1-\bar\alpha_t}}\)
Score \(\|\nabla_{x_t}\log p(x_t) - s_\theta(x_t)\|_2^2\) \(\nabla_{x_t}\log p(x_t) = -\frac{\epsilon_t}{\sqrt{1-\bar\alpha_t}}\)

All three are the same ELBO up to a time-dependent weight; they differ only in what the network is asked to output.

4. Guided Diffusion Models for Conditional Generation

So far the model generates samples from \(p(x_0)\) with no control over what is generated. Now suppose the data comes in pairs

\[ (x_0,\ y), \qquad y : \text{conditioning variable}. \]

Example: class labels

\(x_0\): an image, \(y\): its class label.

Example: text

\(x_0\): an image, \(y\): a text embedding of its caption.

Goal: sample from the conditional distribution \(p(x_0\mid y)\).
Question: how do we modify a DDPM to sample from \(p(x_0\mid y)\)?

5. Classifier Guidance

Recall that DDPMs are score predictors: they predict the true score of the unconditional (marginal) distribution, \(\nabla_{x_t}\log p(x_t)\).

From a conditional sampling perspective, the goal is to predict, or regress over, the conditional score \(\nabla_{x_t}\log p(x_t\mid y)\). Using Bayes' rule,

\[ \begin{aligned} \nabla_{x_t}\log p(x_t\mid y) &= \nabla_{x_t}\log\left(\frac{p(x_t)\,p(y\mid x_t)}{p(y)}\right) \\[6pt] &= \nabla_{x_t}\log p(x_t) + \nabla_{x_t}\log p(y\mid x_t) - \underbrace{\nabla_{x_t}\log p(y)}_{=\,0}. \end{aligned} \]

The last term vanishes because \(p(y)\) does not depend on \(x_t\).

Conditional score = unconditional score + classifier gradient
\[ \boxed{ \nabla_{x_t}\log p(x_t\mid y) = \underbrace{\nabla_{x_t}\log p(x_t)}_{\text{unconditional score}} + \underbrace{\nabla_{x_t}\log p(y\mid x_t)}_{\text{classifier gradient}} } \]

Unconditional score

Comes from the ordinary DDPM U-Net trained with \(\|\epsilon_t - \hat\epsilon_\theta\|_2^2\), since \(\hat\epsilon_\theta\) is a scaled score estimate.

Classifier gradient

Comes from a separate, pre-trained classifier/regressor \(p(y\mid x_t)\), differentiated with respect to its input \(x_t\).

U-Net \(\theta\) \(x_t, t \to \hat\epsilon_\theta\) (unconditional score)
+
Classifier \(p(y\mid x_t)\) pre-trained; backprop to \(x_t\) gives \(\nabla_{x_t}\log p(y\mid x_t)\)
→
\(\nabla_{x_t}\log p(x_t\mid y)\) guided score

The classifier/regressor is trained separately to predict \(y\) (a class label or an embedding) from the noisy input \(x_t\). The two scores are added at every reverse step. Written in terms of the noise prediction, using score \(= -\epsilon/\sqrt{1-\bar\alpha_t}\):

\[ \tilde\epsilon_\theta(x_t, y) = \hat\epsilon_\theta(x_t) - \sqrt{1-\bar\alpha_t}\;\nabla_{x_t}\log p(y\mid x_t), \]

and \(\tilde\epsilon_\theta\) is used in place of \(\hat\epsilon_\theta\) during sampling.

The drawback: the classifier must work on noisy inputs \(x_t\) at every noise level, so an off-the-shelf classifier trained on clean images cannot be used. Training a classifier at different noise levels is difficult.

6. Classifier-Free Guidance

Classifier-free guidance removes the need for a separate classifier. Start from the same decomposition:

\[ \nabla\log p(x_t\mid y) = \nabla\log p(x_t) + \nabla\log p(y\mid x_t). \]

By Bayes' law, the classifier gradient can itself be written using only conditional and unconditional scores:

\[ \nabla\log p(y\mid x_t) = \nabla\log p(x_t\mid y) - \nabla\log p(x_t). \]

Substituting this back, and scaling the classifier part by a weight \(\lambda\) to control how strongly we steer towards \(y\):

\[ \nabla\log p(x_t\mid y) \approx \nabla\log p(x_t) + \lambda\left(\nabla\log p(x_t\mid y) - \nabla\log p(x_t)\right). \]
Classifier-free guidance
\[ \boxed{ \nabla_{x_t}\log p(x_t\mid y) = \lambda\,\nabla_{x_t}\log p(x_t\mid y) + (1-\lambda)\,\nabla_{x_t}\log p(x_t) } \]

\(\lambda\) is a hyperparameter (the guidance scale). \(\lambda = 1\) gives the plain conditional model; \(\lambda > 1\) pushes samples harder towards the condition \(y\).

6.1 Getting both scores from one U-Net

Both terms come from the same network, which takes \(y\) as an extra input:

Conditional pass

\((x_t,\ t,\ y) \to\) U-Net \(\theta\)

Regressor over \(\nabla_{x_t}\log p(x_t\mid y)\).

Unconditional pass

\((x_t,\ t,\ y=\varnothing) \to\) U-Net \(\theta\) (same weights)

Feeding a null label / empty text \(\varnothing\) makes it a regressor over \(\nabla_{x_t}\log p(x_t)\).

During training, \(y\) is randomly replaced by \(\varnothing\) for a fraction of the examples, so the single network learns both scores. During sampling, we run both passes and add the \(\lambda\)-weighted conditional score and the \((1-\lambda)\)-weighted unconditional score before taking the reverse step. In terms of noise predictions:

\[ \tilde\epsilon_\theta(x_t, y) = \lambda\,\hat\epsilon_\theta(x_t, t, y) + (1-\lambda)\,\hat\epsilon_\theta(x_t, t, \varnothing). \]
Why it works: the difference \(\nabla\log p(x_t\mid y) - \nabla\log p(x_t)\) is an implicit classifier gradient: it is the direction that makes \(x_t\) more like \(y\) specifically, rather than more like data in general. No noisy classifier is ever trained.

7. Latent Diffusion Models

The basic idea: build diffusion models on the latent space induced by another encoder–decoder model.

Given data \(x_0 \in \mathbb{R}^d \sim p(x_0)\), the first step is to learn a latent representation \(z_0 \in \mathbb{R}^k\), with \(k \ll d\), using an encoder–decoder model. For example, construct a VQ-VAE to learn a latent space:

\(x_0\) data, \(\mathbb{R}^d\)
→
Enc \(E_\phi\)
→
\(z_0\) latent, \(\mathbb{R}^k\)
→
Dec \(D_\theta\)
→
\(\hat{x}_0\) reconstruction

Let \(E_\phi\) and \(D_\theta\) be the encoder and decoder functions. The trained \(E_{\phi^*}\) and \(D_{\theta^*}\) are learned from the original data \(x_0\), or from some other dataset similar to \(x_0\).

7.1 Training and sampling

  1. All the data points in \(\mathbb{R}^d\) are projected onto the latent space as \(z_0 = E_{\phi^*}(x_0)\).
  2. A DDPM is constructed on the space of \(z_0\): the forward and reverse processes run on \(z_0, z_1, \ldots, z_T\) instead of on pixels.
  3. During inference, to generate a novel point from \(p(x_0)\), first sample a novel latent \(z_{\text{novel}}\) with reverse diffusion, then decode it:
\[ \boxed{ x_{\text{novel}} = D_{\theta^*}(z_{\text{novel}}) \sim p(x_0) } \]
\(z_T\) \(\mathcal{N}(0,I)\)
→
Reverse diffusion in latent space
→
\(z_{\text{novel}}\)
→
\(D_{\theta^*}\) decoder
→
\(x_{\text{novel}}\)
Why bother? Every reverse step needs a forward pass of the U-Net. Running those \(T\) passes on a small \(k\)-dimensional latent instead of a \(d\)-dimensional image is much cheaper, while the decoder takes care of fine pixel detail. This is the idea behind Stable Diffusion.

8. Denoising Diffusion Implicit Models (DDIM): Motivation

DDPMs have two practical problems:

  1. Slow sampling. Generating one sample takes \(T \approx 1000\) network evaluations.
  2. No deterministic posterior inference. The forward process is stochastic: \[ x_t = \sqrt{\bar\alpha_t}\,x_0 + \sqrt{1-\bar\alpha_t}\,\epsilon_t, \qquad \epsilon_t \sim \mathcal{N}(0, I). \] Given an \(x_0\), running the forward process multiple times leads to different \(x_T\) (final latent representations of \(x_0\)). So there does not exist a single, unique \(x_T\) that generates a given \(x_0\) through the reverse diffusion process.
DDIMs solve both issues: slow sampling and non-unique invertibility.

9. A Non-Markovian Family of Forward Processes

Consider a family of inference distributions, parameterized by a vector \(\sigma \in \mathbb{R}_{\ge 0}^{T}\):

\[ q_\sigma(x_{1:T}\mid x_0) \triangleq q_\sigma(x_T\mid x_0)\prod_{t=2}^{T} q_\sigma(x_{t-1}\mid x_t, x_0), \]

where

\[ q_\sigma(x_T\mid x_0) \triangleq \mathcal{N}\!\left(\sqrt{\bar\alpha_T}\,x_0,\ (1-\bar\alpha_T)\,I\right), \]

and, for all \(t > 1\),

The DDIM posterior
\[ \boxed{ q_\sigma(x_{t-1}\mid x_t, x_0) \triangleq \mathcal{N}\!\left( \sqrt{\bar\alpha_{t-1}}\,x_0 + \sqrt{1-\bar\alpha_{t-1}-\sigma_t^2}\cdot \frac{x_t - \sqrt{\bar\alpha_t}\,x_0}{\sqrt{1-\bar\alpha_t}},\ \sigma_t^2 I \right) } \]
Reading the mean: \(\frac{x_t - \sqrt{\bar\alpha_t}\,x_0}{\sqrt{1-\bar\alpha_t}}\) is exactly the noise \(\epsilon_t\) that turned \(x_0\) into \(x_t\). So \(x_{t-1}\) is built as “signal at level \(t-1\)” plus “the same noise direction, rescaled”, plus fresh noise of variance \(\sigma_t^2\).

9.1 The corresponding forward process

The forward process corresponding to this posterior follows from Bayes' rule:

\[ q_\sigma(x_t\mid x_{t-1}, x_0) = \frac{q_\sigma(x_{t-1}\mid x_t, x_0)\;q_\sigma(x_t\mid x_0)}{q_\sigma(x_{t-1}\mid x_0)}. \]

This forward process is not Markovian: each \(x_t\) depends on \(x_0\) as well as \(x_{t-1}\),

\[ x_t \not\perp x_0 \mid x_{t-1}. \]
Key property

In the above process,

\[ \boxed{ q_\sigma(x_t\mid x_0) = \mathcal{N}\!\left(x_t;\ \sqrt{\bar\alpha_t}\,x_0,\ (1-\bar\alpha_t)\,I\right) } \]

which is the same as in a DDPM.

10. Same Marginals, Same ELBO, Same Training

Since \(q_\sigma(x_t\mid x_0)\), the conditional of \(x_t\) given \(x_0\), is the same in both DDPM and DDIM, the ELBOs for both of them are the same, and optimizing the ELBO leads to the same solution.

Why: the training objective of a DDPM/DDIM depends only on \(q(x_t\mid x_0)\): we sample \(x_t\) from it and regress onto the noise. As long as \(q(x_t\mid x_0)\) is the same as that of DDPMs, any process can be used, and it leads to the same solution of the ELBO. In other words, any definition of \(q(x_{1:T}\mid x_0)\) can be chosen, provided its marginals \(q(x_t\mid x_0)\) match the DDPM.

10.1 Defining the decoder (reverse process)

The reverse process plugs a prediction of \(x_0\) into the DDIM posterior:

\[ p_\theta(x_{t-1}\mid x_t) = \begin{cases} \mathcal{N}\!\left(\hat{x}_\theta(x_1),\ \sigma_1^2 I\right) & t = 1, \\[4pt] q_\sigma\!\left(x_{t-1}\mid x_t,\ \hat{x}_\theta(x_t)\right) & \text{otherwise}, \end{cases} \]

where the approximation of \(x_0\) comes from the trained noise predictor:

\[ \hat{x}_\theta(x_t) = \frac{x_t - \sqrt{1-\bar\alpha_t}\,\hat\epsilon_\theta(x_t)}{\sqrt{\bar\alpha_t}} \quad : \text{approx. for } x_0. \]

This is Eq. (1) again: recall \(x_t = \sqrt{\bar\alpha_t}\,x_0 + \sqrt{1-\bar\alpha_t}\,\epsilon_t\), so \(x_0 = (x_t - \sqrt{1-\bar\alpha_t}\,\epsilon_t)/\sqrt{\bar\alpha_t}\), and we replace the true noise with its prediction.

The main result

The ELBO of the non-Markovian family above and that of the DDPM are the same (up to a constant).

\[ \boxed{ \text{Training the regression network in a DDPM is implicitly training all of the above non-Markovian models.} } \]
Consequence: there is no difference between training a DDPM and a DDIM. Only the inference process is different. Any DDPM checkpoint can be sampled with DDIM, without retraining.

11. Inference with DDIMs

With the trained network \(\theta^*\), sampling from \(p_{\theta^*}(x_{t-1}\mid x_t)\) means sampling from

\[ p_{\theta^*}(x_{t-1}\mid x_t) = \begin{cases} \mathcal{N}\!\left(\hat{x}_{\theta^*}(x_1),\ \sigma_1^2 I\right) & t = 1, \\[4pt] q_\sigma\!\left(x_{t-1}\mid x_t,\ \hat{x}_{\theta^*}(x_t)\right) & \text{otherwise}. \end{cases} \]

From the definition of \(\hat{x}_{\theta^*}\), the noise term inside the DDIM posterior is simply the predicted noise:

\[ \hat{x}_{\theta^*}(x_t) = \frac{x_t - \sqrt{1-\bar\alpha_t}\,\hat\epsilon_{\theta^*}}{\sqrt{\bar\alpha_t}} \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \frac{x_t - \sqrt{\bar\alpha_t}\,\hat{x}_{\theta^*}}{\sqrt{1-\bar\alpha_t}} = \hat\epsilon_{\theta^*}. \]

Substituting into the DDIM posterior,

\[ q_\sigma\!\left(x_{t-1}\mid x_t,\ \hat{x}_{\theta^*}\right) = \mathcal{N}\!\left( \sqrt{\bar\alpha_{t-1}}\,\hat{x}_{\theta^*} + \sqrt{1-\bar\alpha_{t-1}-\sigma_t^2}\cdot \frac{x_t - \sqrt{\bar\alpha_t}\,\hat{x}_{\theta^*}}{\sqrt{1-\bar\alpha_t}},\ \sigma_t^2 I \right). \]

Putting all of the above together, sampling from \(p_{\theta^*}(x_{t-1}\mid x_t)\) is as follows:

The general DDIM update
\[ \boxed{ x_{t-1} = \sqrt{\bar\alpha_{t-1}} \underbrace{\left[\frac{x_t - \sqrt{1-\bar\alpha_t}\,\hat\epsilon_{\theta^*}}{\sqrt{\bar\alpha_t}}\right]}_{\text{predicted } x_0} + \underbrace{\sqrt{1-\bar\alpha_{t-1}-\sigma_t^2}\;\hat\epsilon_{\theta^*}}_{\text{direction pointing to } x_t} + \underbrace{\sigma_t\,z}_{\text{random noise}}, \qquad z \sim \mathcal{N}(0, I) } \]

12. From DDPM to DDIM: Choosing \(\sigma_t\)

12.1 \(\sigma_t\) that recovers the DDPM

When

\[ \sigma_t = \sqrt{\frac{1-\bar\alpha_{t-1}}{1-\bar\alpha_t}}\; \sqrt{1-\frac{\bar\alpha_t}{\bar\alpha_{t-1}}}, \]

the above random process reduces to the DDPM. Since \(\bar\alpha_t/\bar\alpha_{t-1} = \alpha_t\), this is \(\sigma_t^2 = \frac{(1-\alpha_t)(1-\bar\alpha_{t-1})}{1-\bar\alpha_t}\), exactly the posterior variance \(\sigma_q^2\) from Part 1.

12.2 \(\sigma_t = 0\): the DDIM

When \(\sigma_t = 0\), the sampling process has the following form:

Deterministic DDIM sampling
\[ \boxed{ x_{t-1} = \sqrt{\bar\alpha_{t-1}} \left[\frac{x_t - \sqrt{1-\bar\alpha_t}\,\hat\epsilon_{\theta^*}}{\sqrt{\bar\alpha_t}}\right] + \sqrt{1-\bar\alpha_{t-1}}\;\hat\epsilon_{\theta^*} } \]
  • The above sampling process is deterministic. The only randomness is from the starting point \(x_T \sim \mathcal{N}(0, I)\).
  • With \(\sigma_t = 0\), the model is called a DDIM.
  • The forward process in DDIM with \(\sigma_t = 0\) is also deterministic.
DDPM DDIM
\(\sigma_t\) \(\sigma_q\) (posterior std. dev.) \(0\)
Forward process Markovian, stochastic Non-Markovian, deterministic
Sampling Stochastic at every step Deterministic given \(x_T\)
\(x_0 \to x_T\) mapping Not unique Unique (invertible)
Training Identical: \(\|\epsilon_t - \hat\epsilon_\theta(x_t)\|_2^2\)
DDIM inversion: because both directions are deterministic, DDIM has a unique invertibility. A real image \(x_0\) can be mapped to a single latent \(x_T\), and decoding that \(x_T\) gives the same image back. This is what makes image editing with diffusion models possible: invert, modify, regenerate.
Faster sampling: since training only uses the marginals \(q(x_t\mid x_0)\), the deterministic update can also be run on a short subsequence of time steps (for example, 50 out of 1000), jumping from \(\bar\alpha_t\) straight to \(\bar\alpha_{t'}\) for some \(t' < t - 1\). This is how DDIM addresses the slow sampling of DDPMs.

13. Summary

  1. Noise regression: writing \(\mu_q\) in terms of \(\epsilon_t\) turns the consistency term into \(\|\epsilon_t - \hat\epsilon_\theta(x_t)\|_2^2\).
  2. Score prediction: Tweedie's formula gives \(x_0 = (x_t + (1-\bar\alpha_t)\nabla\log p(x_t))/\sqrt{\bar\alpha_t}\), so the network can equally regress onto the score.
  3. Score = scaled noise: \(\nabla_{x_t}\log p(x_t) = -\epsilon_t/\sqrt{1-\bar\alpha_t}\); a noise predictor is implicitly a score predictor.
  4. Classifier guidance: conditional score = unconditional score + \(\nabla_{x_t}\log p(y\mid x_t)\) from a separately trained noisy classifier.
  5. Classifier-free guidance: \(\lambda\,\nabla\log p(x_t\mid y) + (1-\lambda)\,\nabla\log p(x_t)\), both from one U-Net, using a null condition \(y=\varnothing\) for the unconditional score.
  6. Latent diffusion: diffuse in the latent space \(z_0 = E_{\phi^*}(x_0)\) of a pre-trained encoder–decoder; decode with \(D_{\theta^*}\).
  7. DDIM: a non-Markovian forward family with the same \(q(x_t\mid x_0)\), hence the same ELBO and training; with \(\sigma_t = 0\), sampling is deterministic and invertible.
The central equations
\[ \boxed{ \nabla_{x_t}\log p(x_t) = -\frac{\epsilon_t}{\sqrt{1-\bar\alpha_t}} } \qquad \boxed{ \nabla_{x_t}\log p(x_t\mid y) = \nabla_{x_t}\log p(x_t) + \nabla_{x_t}\log p(y\mid x_t) } \] \[ \boxed{ x_{t-1} = \sqrt{\bar\alpha_{t-1}}\,\hat{x}_{\theta^*}(x_t) + \sqrt{1-\bar\alpha_{t-1}-\sigma_t^2}\;\hat\epsilon_{\theta^*} + \sigma_t\,z } \]

A single trained noise predictor is therefore much more than a denoiser: it is a score model that can be steered with guidance, run in a compressed latent space, and sampled deterministically with DDIM.
